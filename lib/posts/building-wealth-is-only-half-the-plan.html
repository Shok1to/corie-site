Most conversations about personal finance start in the same place: how to grow your money. People compare RRSP and TFSA strategies, track market returns, and calculate how much they need to retire comfortably.

Far fewer stop to ask a simpler, more urgent question: What keeps that plan intact if life interrupts your ability to earn?

In financial planning, building wealth and protecting wealth are not separate conversations. Without a protective foundation, a single unexpected health crisis or premature death can force a family to liquidate investments at the worst possible time, drain years of savings, or take on debt just to cover everyday bills.

Why protection gets overlooked

It is easy to understand why investing feels more engaging than insurance. Watching an account balance grow feels like progress; paying a monthly insurance premium feels like an expense for something you hope never happens.

Yet the math works the other way around. For most households, your single largest financial asset is not your home or your investment portfolio—it is your ability to earn an income over the coming decades. When that paycheque stops early, savings disappear fast. Nearly 30% of Canadians report their savings would run out within six months of a major health setback.

Government programs like Employment Insurance (EI) sickness benefits or CPP disability offer a basic safety net, and workplace benefits help, but neither is designed to replace your full take-home pay or cover major lump-sum costs. Personal insurance exists to bridge that gap.

The four core types of personal insurance, explained simply

You do not need to memorize industry jargon to understand how insurance protects your household. Each major type of coverage solves a specific financial problem.

Type of InsuranceHow It Pays OutWhat Problem It Solves
Term Life Insurance A tax-free lump sum to your beneficiaries if you pass away during the policy term Replaces lost income so your family can pay the mortgage, cover living costs, and fund future goals
Permanent Life Insurance A tax-free lump sum whenever you pass away (lifetime coverage) Covers final expenses, estate taxes, or legacy goals that do not expire after 10 or 20 years
Disability Insurance A monthly cheque (typically 60%–70% of income) if illness or injury stops you from working Keeps the household running—paying rent, groceries, and bills—when your regular paycheque stops
Critical Illness Insurance A one-time, tax-free lump sum if you are diagnosed with a covered condition (such as cancer, heart attack, or stroke) Gives you immediate cash for out-of-pocket medical costs, travel, home care, or allowing a spouse to take time off work

1. Life insurance: Protecting the people who rely on you

If anyone depends on your income—a spouse, children, or aging parents—life insurance ensures they are not left carrying financial obligations alone.

  • Term life insurance covers you for a set window, such as 10, 20, or 30 years. It is straightforward, affordable, and ideal for temporary responsibilities like raising children or paying down a mortgage.
  • Permanent life insurance lasts for life and often includes a cash-value component. It is typically used for lifelong dependents, estate preservation, or tax-efficient wealth transfer.

2. Disability insurance: Protecting your paycheque

Many people assume disability only happens in high-risk jobs, yet most long-term claims stem from everyday health issues like back injuries, mental health conditions, or chronic illness. Because you are far more likely to experience a multi-month disability before age 65 than to pass away prematurely, disability coverage acts as a monthly replacement paycheque while you recover.

3. Critical illness insurance: Buying breathing room during a diagnosis

A serious medical diagnosis brings expenses that provincial health care does not cover—from specialized medications and travel to lost income when a partner steps back from work to care for you. Critical illness insurance pays a lump sum upon diagnosis and survival of a waiting period, and you can spend that money however you choose.

4. Extended health and dental coverage: Managing everyday gaps

Provincial health plans cover hospital visits and doctors, but not routine prescription drugs, dental work, vision care, or physiotherapy. If you are self-employed, retiring early, or between jobs, personal health and dental coverage keeps routine and unexpected medical bills from eroding your monthly budget.

How protection and wealth work together

Think of your financial plan like a house. Investments, registered accounts, and tax strategies are the upper floors; they build long-term value. Insurance is the foundation that keeps the structure standing when a storm hits.

When the right coverage is in place:

  • Your investments stay invested. You are not forced to sell your TFSA or cash out an RRSP, and trigger avoidable taxes, during a market downturn just to pay the mortgage.
  • Your family keeps its choices. A surviving spouse or recovering earner has time to grieve, heal, and make calm decisions rather than rushed financial ones.
  • Your retirement stays on track. Even a multi-year health setback does not have to derail decades of careful saving.

Where to start

You do not need every policy on the shelf, nor should you buy coverage without knowing what you already have. Start with four practical steps:

  1. Review your workplace benefits. Check how much life and disability coverage your employer provides, whether it covers bonuses or commissions, and whether it disappears if you change jobs.
  2. Identify your non-negotiable expenses. Add up what your household needs each month for housing, debt payments, childcare, and basic living costs.
  3. Match the policy to the risk. Use temporary coverage for temporary debts, and make sure your income itself is protected, not just your life.
  4. Revisit as life changes. A new home, a growing family, a business launch, or a major raise are all moments to verify your protection still fits.
