Most of us plan for retirement. Far fewer plan for the day income stops early because of an illness, an injury or a death in the family. That day is more common than it feels, and the protection against it usually costs less than people expect.

How likely is it, really?

The Canadian Life and Health Insurance Association (CLHIA) says 1 in 3 people will be disabled for 90 days or more at least once before age 65. CLHIA statistics reported by Investment Executive suggest a 20-year-old man is about three times more likely to be disabled for at least 90 days than to die before 65. For most working people, the likelier threat to the paycheque is not death. It is a long stretch of being unable to work.

Yet we protect against it the least. RBC Insurance reports that nearly 30% of Canadians say their savings would run out within six months of a major health setback, while only about 10% have disability insurance.

Life insurance shows a similar gap. A 2023 LIMRA and Life Happens study found 57% of Canadian adults have life insurance, but 31% (8.4 million adults) say they need it or need more. About 4 in 10 said their family would face financial hardship within six months if the main earner died unexpectedly.

What public programs actually pay

Canada’s safety net helps, but it was never designed to replace a full income.

  • EI sickness benefits pay 55% of your average insurable weekly earnings, to a maximum of $729 a week in 2026, for up to 26 weeks. On a $60,000 salary, that works out to roughly $635 a week before tax (our calculation), and then it ends.
  • CPP disability can pay up to about $1,741 a month in 2026, but the actual amount depends on your contribution history and is not guaranteed. Eligibility rules are strict.
  • Group benefits at work help, but check the fine print. Group life is typically set at one to two times base salary, and group long-term disability is usually calculated on base salary only, which can leave out bonuses or commissions.

Meanwhile, the mortgage, rent, childcare and groceries do not shrink.

So are people turning to GoFundMe because they lack insurance?

Partly, but the research cannot prove it, and the picture differs between the U.S. and Canada.

In the United States, medical crowdfunding is largely about medical bills. Researchers found that nearly 27% of GoFundMe campaigns between 2010 and 2018 sought money for health care costs, and that the median medical goal was $50,000 in the U.S. versus $19,000 in Canada. A cross-country study in JAMA Network Open found about 80% of U.S. medical campaigns were for routine health care, compared with 22% in Canada.

In Canada, hospital and physician care is largely covered, so campaigns tend to fill other holes. The JAMA Network Open authors noted that the popularity of medical crowdfunding in countries with universal care cannot be explained the same way as in the U.S. Simon Fraser University researchers reviewed 423 health-related GoFundMe campaigns in Metro Vancouver and found that the most common motivation, at about 34%, was replacing wages lost to illness. Others involved prescription drugs, dental care, medical devices, home accessibility and travel for treatment. The lead author told The Province that many of these campaigners don’t qualify for EI or other support.

The wider trend points the same way. GoFundMe reported a 274% rise in Canadian campaigns mentioning cost of living since 2020, and CBC Radio reported in June 2026 on more than 15,000 fundraisers for essential needs, including a man with a serious infection and no income who started a campaign to keep his housing.

A fair reading: these studies look at what campaigns ask for. They do not measure whether the organizers had insurance. But the requests are exactly what disability, critical illness and life insurance exist to cover: lost income, uncovered costs and final expenses.

The same is true after a death. GoFundMe has raised $340 million across 113,000 funeral campaigns, about $3,000 each (2016 figures). A U.S. industry commentary noted that the average funeral campaign on one platform raised about $2,000, against a $7,000-plus average funeral. These are older U.S. numbers, but the pattern holds: the money raised often falls short of what a policy would have paid.

Why a campaign isn’t a plan

There is nothing wrong with asking your community for help, and many people are generous. It just makes a poor primary plan.

  • Results are uneven. In one study of U.S., U.K. and Canadian campaigns, Black organizers raised 11.5% less and women 5.9% less than other organizers. In Metro Vancouver, campaigns in one health region often raised more than those in another.
  • The amount is unknown. You find out what you raised only after the crisis has started.
  • It asks you to go public at your most vulnerable moment.

Protection costs less than you think

LIMRA found that 53% of Canadians who know they need coverage say they have not bought it because they believe it is too expensive. Here are some illustrative numbers.

ProtectionExampleApproximate cost
Term life, $500,000, 20-year term Healthy non-smoker, age 30 About $20/month (women) or $28/month (men)
Disability, $3,000/month benefit to age 65, 90-day wait 35-year-old male non-smoker, office administrator About $67/month
Disability, general rule of thumb Individual long-term policy Often 1% to 3% of annual income, or $1,000 to $3,000 a year on $100,000

In the examples above, both policies together come to roughly $90 to $95 a month, a small fraction of the income they protect.

Two honest caveats. First, these figures are illustrative. Your price depends on age, health, occupation, smoking status, the waiting period and the benefit period, and Canada Life notes premiums can range from 1% to 9% of salary. A longer waiting period lowers the premium. Second, many of these sources are insurers, brokers and industry groups, who benefit from selling coverage. Treat them as a guide, and get real quotes before deciding.

Where to start

  1. Find out what you already have at work. What multiple of salary is your group life? What percentage does long-term disability replace? Does coverage end if you leave?
  2. Add up the bills that must keep being paid. Housing, debts, childcare, food, insurance.
  3. Count your cushion. How many months could savings and public benefits cover? The gap is what insurance is for.
  4. Get quotes, then revisit. Review your coverage after big life events such as a new home, a child or a raise.
